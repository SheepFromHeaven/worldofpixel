Birds

Birds are the first animals here that leave the ground. They are populations of the food web like the reptiles: every patch holds eggs, nestlings, fledglings and adults, birds eat insects, seeds, fruit, frogs, lizards, fish and each other's eggs, and are eaten in turn by raptors, snakes, crows and gulls. What the map shows is that population: a few of each patch's birds, perched, hopping, circling or paddling, each with a shadow on the ground that separates from it as it climbs.

2Habitat, the food web, and what is drawn

  1. HabitatFour numbers per tile: tree, open, water, edge. Land, fresh water and the sea fill them differently, so one table of tastes serves all three media. A bird flies over any of them but lives and lands only in its realm.
  2. NestsEach species also has a taste for nest sites over the same four numbers: tit and woodpecker want trees, pheasants open ground, mallards reed banks, kingfishers a bank. Eggs and nestlings live only on land and are as many as the nest sites in the patch allow.
  3. PopulationThe food web keeps the eggs, nestlings, fledglings and adults of each species in each patch. Birds that eat insects compete with frogs and lizards; raptors take frogs, lizards and small birds; crows, gulls and snakes take eggs. Migrants are not scripted: the swallow and the stork cannot stand the cold, so they die back in a cold patch and the animals that disperse from the warm south refill it.
  4. SlotsThe ground is cut into 10 m cells with 80 slots in each, the species' slot counts laid end to end (one for the eagle, four for the sparrow). A slot wakes when its fixed random number is under the density the patch holds, spread over the places the species like. A fledgling is drawn at 62% of adult size, nestlings are not drawn.
  5. FlocksStarlings, gulls and macaws carry the flock trait: the slots of one cell share their timing and their destinations, offset by a few metres, so they take off, cross and land together.

A click is a scare, not a hunt. A startled bird takes off and flies away, stays away for about half a minute and flies back. The food web holds no individual birds, so one that flees has not been eaten or fed.

7Migration

Migration is a movement of the stocks in the food web, not an animation. A migratory species has a strength from 0 to 1 (migrant: swallow, stork, goose and crane 1, hummingbird 0.8, swan 0.6, starling, pelican and snowy owl 0.5, mallard and hawk 0.4, eagle, kestrel, gull, flamingo, cormorant 0.3). Each step, when adults and fledglings drift between two neighbouring patches, a migrant weights each patch's attraction by how comfortable its air is now ((0.02 + comfort)^3, mixed in by the strength) and moves faster the bigger the difference in comfort (up to six times). The climate the food web reads includes the season, so when the north cools the animals flow toward the warmer patches and when it warms again they flow back, and a stock that stays behind is killed by the cold. The breeding range follows because nests and food are in the patch, not the animal.

On the map individual migrants change home and fly there. A migratory species gives a share of its slots to migration (the strength, taken from the last slot down), and each such slot has a summer home in its cell and a winter home 180 to 320 m away along the meridian, toward the equator, in the hemisphere it was born in. The year decides where it is: in the weeks from 0.48 to 0.50 of the year (spring and autumn mirrored for the south, which has its own half-year phase) each stream of birds picks its own moment to leave, flies the whole distance at its cruise speed (at least 9 m/s, so a trip takes half a minute at 1x) and settles into the new home, where it wanders as it always did. In the air it is high, fast and fearless, takes off and lands on the ground (the altitude and the shadow follow), and nothing is stored: the position is a function of the clock, the cell and the season. Because the lattice of birds slides with the migration, every bird is still drawn by exactly one cell, so none is lost or doubled on the way. Whether it is drawn at all is the food web's answer at its origin and its destination, mixed by how far along it is with no floor under the stock, so a winter home fills as fast as the food web fills it. Birds are sorted against the trees by height through the same depth buffer the props write: a bird perched or hopping is covered by a canopy over it, a flyer crosses over the forest, and one at about a tree's height passes over a low tree and under a tall one.

8How each bird moves and looks

  • Hops and flies (sparrow, robin, tit, starling, woodpecker, macaw, hummingbird, kingfisher, gull, cormorant, owl): sits for its rest time, then flies in a gentle bow to a new spot inside its reach and sits again. Altitude is an arc over the flight, so the shadow slides away from the bird and back.
  • Hawks on the wing (swallow): almost always in flight, short flights with hardly a pause, at a low steady height.
  • Soars (kestrel, hawk, eagle, vulture): circles a drifting centre at height, never landing, wings held out and barely moving, the shadow far down the screen.
  • Swims (mallard, swan, pelican, penguin): paddles slowly on water or walks on land, leaving a ripple ring behind it; a bird that lands on water, any species, throws a ring.
  • Walks (pigeon, crow, turkey, peacock, chicken, goose, crane, flamingo, heron, pheasant, stork): moves a few metres at a time on foot and flies only when startled.
  • Flightless (emu, ostrich, penguin): never leaves the ground, wings folded; startled, it runs at its own run speed instead of taking off, and the penguin swims and dives from sea ice and the coast.
  • Display (peacock, turkey): now and then, standing still, the tail opens into a fan of eyespots.
  • Hovering (hummingbird): hangs in the air with its wings blurred through a dither for most of the time between perches.
  • Startled: a click throws a ripple and every bird inside its flush distance takes off a moment apart, flies 2.6 times that far away, waits, and flies back. Raptors and big waders flush from far, swallows never.

Every bird is a distance field on the world's own texel grid, not a sprite: a body ellipse, a head, a bill, a tail (fan, fork or plume) and two wings. In flight the wings sweep and beat (the beat rate is the species'; big birds beat in bursts between glides) and are foreshortened by the cosine of the beat angle, as they would be seen from above. Lit from the top-left in steps, with an inked edge and a Bayer dither fade. Shadows are drawn in the ground layer and never over water; bodies are sorted with the plants by their height, so a bird under a canopy is covered by it and a bird above one is seen over it.
