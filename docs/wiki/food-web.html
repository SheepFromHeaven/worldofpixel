The food web

The insects, amphibians, reptiles and fish are no longer scenery drawn from a habitat map. They are populations that live in the world's patches (16 by 16 tiles), eat each other and the plants, lay eggs, hatch, grow up and die, and the numbers you see are the ones this simulation keeps. A year in it is 1 game day, so a game minute is a good part of a season. Nothing is a fixed quota: a big lizard is rare because it needs a great deal of food and a great deal of ground, and a fish is eaten in numbers its own breeding can bear.

1How it works

  1. PatchesThe world is cut into 16 tile patches, the same cells as the weather. Each keeps, for every species, how many eggs, hatchlings, juveniles and adults are in it. Habitat is read once from the world (water, shelter, open warm ground, banks) and averaged over a patch, water-bound stages from an exact count of the pond tiles.
  2. ClimateEach tick the patch takes its temperature from the yearly climate without the daily swing (animals shelter from the day's extremes), and its snow cover from the weather. A cold or hot patch slows feeding and development, and an extreme one kills.
  3. DietWho can eat whom is not typed in. A species names the kinds it eats (insects, amphibians, reptiles, fish, game, plants) and a window of prey size relative to its own, and every stage of every species that fits, in the same medium (land, fresh water, sea), is on the menu with a weight that peaks at the middle of the window. A hatchling's window is relative to its own small mass, and a young carnivore also takes insects.
  4. EatingA predator's supply is the prey it can reach (each stock times how easily that kind is caught, less a refuge of 3% of its territory capacity that nobody takes, and less again when it is scarce, so a predator does not hunt the last of anything). Intake is 2 x supply / (supply + need), so it is the supply per mouth that decides: full below one, a feast above.
  5. Breeding and growthSurplus intake becomes eggs, up to a clutch a female can lay, only when the patch is warm enough and has a nest site. Eggs hatch with warmth, hatchlings and juveniles grow at a pace set by how well they eat, and an adult dies of old age, hunger, crowding, or what eats it.
  6. MovingAdults and juveniles drift toward neighbouring patches that offer more (suitable and well fed), so a booming patch spills over and an empty one fills.

Why big animals are rare. Food need grows with mass to the 0.67, so a body ten times heavier needs about five times the food, not ten, but the ground it holds as a territory grows nearly as fast, and a predator's prey is itself a small, scarce stock. A crocodile's territory is hundreds of patches' worth of prey on one animal, and where prey is thinner than that it simply does not breed enough to stay. A predator also ranges over several patches, so its supply is the food around it (up to 6 times a patch's worth, by territory size) while what it removes comes from this patch alone.

2Size is the rule: need, territory, and appetite

An animal's food need is c x mass^0.67 kilograms a year (c is 9 for amphibians, 5 for reptiles, 4 for insects), times an appetite for how it lives: snakes and chameleons wait and eat little (0.3 to 0.4), crocodiles 0.35, big lizards 0.5, small lizards a full 1. The territory it needs is 300 x mass^0.6 square metres. Insects are plant eaters with a fixed biomass a square metre, scaled by the habitat.

3Eggs, hatchlings, juveniles, adults

Every species but the live-bearers (adders, sidewinders, tree boas, anacondas, sea snakes, slow worms, and the game animals) lays eggs, and each stage is a stock of its own in every patch. Eggs are eaten by animals that eat eggs (snakes, monitors, snapping turtles, newts, ants and beetles). The young are fed by what a small animal can take, and amphibian eggs and tadpoles, and the larvae of dragonflies and midges, live in the calm water of the patch, so they need a pond. Turtles and crocodilians lay on the bank, and a sea turtle on a warm beach.

The numbers below are chosen so that a population with plenty of food and room grows by about a factor of four to eight a generation, and the rest is the world: food, crowding, heat and cold, and being eaten. Insects take a larval stage and no juvenile, amphibians have tadpoles and froglets, the big reptiles take years to grow up.

5What the sim does when left alone

This is the real thing run headless on this seed for 16 years (16 game days) in steps of a quarter year, starting from a seed population sized from the food each patch has, with the yearly average climate and no one disturbing it. It is also what npm run foodweb prints.

The age structure at the end is that of a stable population: many eggs and hatchlings, fewer and fewer of each older stage.

7The fish are in it too

Fish keep their shoals (see the fish page) and now take part. Every shoal also carries eggs and fry: a spawning shoal lays eggs when the water is warm enough, they hatch, the fry grow and join the adults, and the fry are prey for the hunting fish. The fish are also fed and eaten from the patch's side: perch, trout, minnows and carp take tadpoles, frog spawn and insect larvae from the water's patch (up to 25% of what is there in a tick, so they never clear a pond), and every reptile that eats fish takes some from the shoals in its patch. A visible shoal stands for the many fish of the water around it, so what a predator removes is scaled up by 25 from what the shoal loses. The fish are not saved; like every animal here they regrow from the seed.

8What is tuned, and what is not true yet

  • Nothing of this is saved. A saved game rebuilds the animals from the seed and settles them for a couple of years before the first frame, so a long game does not keep its own history.
  • Birds and mammals do not exist. The game stand-in and the insect predators that are not drawn (spiders, birds) are folded into base rates.
  • Constants that set the balance are in DEFAULT_TUNING: how hungry animals are (0.5), how much of what is eaten becomes young (0.3), how many plants there are for the small and the large (450 and 90 kg a patch a year), the insect density multiplier (3). npm run foodweb runs the model headless and prints every species' trend, TUNE='{"hunger":0.7}' overrides them, and DIAG=komodo prints what the best patches offer a species.
  • Small prey and large predators are limited by different things: small animals by food (their numbers follow the insects), big ones by ground (a crocodile's territory), and the middle by who eats them. Seeing a rare animal is meant to take looking.
  • A patch is 16 by 16 tiles. What is drawn inside it is the patch's density spread over the places each species likes, so a frog sits by the water even though the patch holds a pond and a forest.
