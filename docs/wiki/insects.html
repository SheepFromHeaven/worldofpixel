Insects

A bee is still not an entity: it is what the world says should be hovering at that flower, at that hour, in that weather, drawn when you look. But how many of each insect there are is no longer a fixed map. Each insect is a stock in every patch of the food web (eggs, larvae, adults) that eats plants or other insects, breeds, and is eaten by frogs, lizards, chameleons, dragonflies, fish and the young of nearly everything with a backbone, so a meadow full of insectivores is quieter. Each place has four habitat numbers (nectar, foliage, water, ground), each species has a taste for them and a body clock, and the product, scaled by how full the patch is, is how many of it are about. 15 species are in, and a new one is a row here and a line of biology in sim/fauna/life.ts.

Source: src/sim/insects/species.ts, src/sim/fauna/habitat/insectHabitat.ts, src/render/lit/insectPass.ts, src/render/lit/shaders/insects.vert

2How a population becomes insects on the screen

  1. HabitatFour numbers per tile, built once from the world: nectar, foliage, water, ground. They are a texture.
  2. TasteEach species weighs the four. A bee wants nectar, an ant wants bare ground, a midge wants water.
  3. ClockTemperature, season, time of day, wind and rain each scale it from 0 to 1. The same sky the weather and the fish already use.
  4. FillThe food web keeps how full each patch is of each species (its stock over what the habitat could hold). A bee plant patch the predators have thinned is half full, and shows half the bees.
  5. SlotsThe ground is cut into 4 m cells with 749 slots in each. A slot belongs to one species. It is awake when its fixed random number is below the abundance there times the patch's fill.
  6. MotionWhere an awake slot is, and which way it faces, is a function of the clock and the slot. There is no state to store.

This is the whole drawing trick. Because a slot's identity is fixed by its cell and number, the same bee is at the same flower however you arrive, a swarm builds up as dusk falls instead of popping in, and the cost is the number of cells on screen, not the number of insects in the world. Nothing at all is drawn until strip zoom 30, where one metre is 30 pixels. What is kept per patch is a number of eggs, larvae and adults of each species (never per insect), so an insect is never saved either.

Density is a probability, never a count. Abundance (times the fill) is 0 to 1 and a slot wakes when key < abundance, fading in over a small margin so one drifting across the threshold at dusk does not blink. A cell with three honey bee slots and a flower meadow at noon has three bees. The same cell in a gale has none.

4Habitat: four maps of the world

The habitat is computed from what is on the ground, by habitatAt, and uploaded as an RGBA texture the shader reads. It is built a band of sixteen rows a frame, nearest the camera first, so there is no hitch, and rebuilt slowly if the world changes.

5The clock: when each species is out

Wind and rain thin everything out. Each species has a wind limit: above 60% of it a species starts to leave and by the limit it is gone, measured at the ground as 40% of the wind vector, because grass and trees shelter what lives in them. Rain sends most of them home (rain in the table is how much it matters, ants barely mind).

7How each one moves

Every species has one of ten motions, a closed-form path in the slot's own time, so a bee at a flower now and a bee at the same flower one second later are linked by arithmetic, not by memory:

  • Hover and hop (bees): hold a point for a couple of seconds, then dart to the next one, a few metres off.
  • Flutter (butterflies and moths): a slow looping path of two sines, heading read off its velocity, wings opening and closing at about 10 Hz.
  • Skim (dragonflies): hold a station for several seconds, then dart several metres to the next.
  • Swarm (midges): a shared centre per cell, each midge orbiting it at its own speed. They are a cloud because they share a centre.
  • Glow (fireflies): a slow drift and a blink once or twice a second, with a soft halo.
  • Colony (ants, termites): a black wiggling mass of 300 to 400 to a colony, each one drifting slowly and never stopping on a sum of three slow sines of its own speed and phase, with a stepped two pixel jitter, thickest at the mound and thinning out to about half a metre. The mound is a faint, dark, sparsely dithered disc (an ant hill is pine needles and dry leaves, so nearly black), one per cell where the habitat, temperature and season allow a colony, and the same cell always has the same one. Termites want bare, dry ground with no plants or water, which is the desert.
  • Skate (water striders): hold a spot on the water for minutes, then glide a metre or two in about a second, five to ten times a game day, with a ring that spreads from where it stopped (the ring is drawn under the water plants, the strider over them). A click on water (it throws a ripple, with or without a tool) startles every strider within 1 m: each hops 1 to 2 m directly away (give or take 40 degrees) in 0.3 s, a moment apart, and its landing throws its own ring. The last 16 clicks are remembered and each jump is added to the strider's position on its usual schedule, so a second click moves it on from where the first left it, and the hops ease back over about two minutes.
  • Crawl, hop, buzz (beetles, grasshoppers, flies): a long slow walk, a hop, a fly that loops in a small area. Fireflies are a pixel that blinks inside a dithered glow.

An insect is a single chunky pixel in its species colour, snapped to the two pixel grid the rest of the world uses, so the way it moves is the whole of its look: a bee or dragonfly holds a station and then darts to the next, ants keep an even gap along their line, a firefly is a pixel that comes and goes. Ground insects (ants, termites, beetles and the mounds) are drawn under the plants, so a leaf covers them; flying ones are drawn over everything but the clouds.

8What this is for

Because insects are a field, the sim can read the field without ever having had an insect. The fish already live on 50% insects and plankton when no prey is near (FORAGE_FLOOR). Pollination, a spider's catch, a frog's hunting ground, the reason a swarm sits over a still pond in July: each is a question of how much of one species is at a place and time, and abundance answers it for any tile without a single object to keep.
