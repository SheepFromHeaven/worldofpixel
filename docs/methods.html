The Book
of Methods

A town is a set of rigs it has built and a book of methods it has worked out. A method is a recipe with its settings written down: what fuel, how fast, how much air, how long. You get better vessels by understanding that a taller flue pulls more air and that too much air chills a chamber — not by pressing a button at the right moment.

1What changed since draft 1

Draft 1 put a mini-game in front of every craft. It read as an interactive museum: you passed a test once, the band learned the craft, and nothing you did afterwards touched it. Four changes fix that.

Draft 1Draft 2Why
A mini-game per craftA rig with knobsProgress should come from understanding a process, not from dexterity. Nothing is gated on reaction time or precision of motion anywhere in this design.
One score, taught onceA setting you keep tuningA score is a gate you walk through. A setting is a decision that stays live, can be revisited, and can differ between two rigs doing the same job.
Settings belong to a stationSettings belong to a methodNobody built fifteen fires, one per purpose. One kiln runs lime today and brick tomorrow, and the knowledge of how to do each is written down separately from the thing it runs on.
Trials were production runsTrials are a separate, cheap actionExperimenting should be something you choose to spend on, not a side effect of working.

The one thing kept wholesale is knapping, because what was good about it was choosing where to strike, not the clock. Section 10 shows it with the timing taken out.

Two further corrections came out of reading draft 2 back. Knobs evolve as well as readouts — the draft drew continuous sliders and response curves from minute one while also claiming you unlock senses, which cannot both be true; section 11 fixes it. And hunger and thirst start at minute one rather than waiting for a later chapter, because needs are the bottom of the pyramid and everything above them is built on hours they leave over; section 4 is new.

A third came out of playing the first implementation. The map carries work areas, not machines, and a process is a handful of stations standing in one of them rather than a floor of squares with chutes between. Routing a load across a room was never the decision this game is about, and it put a second puzzle in front of the real one. Section 3 is new, and it simplifies the map, the dispatch and the panel all at once.

And one the implementation found on its own, which this draft has taken: needs are three meters on each villager, not a budget for the town. A meter runs down, crosses its mark, and that villager goes to see to it. The day is then counted rather than computed, which is the rule the rest of the document already lives by. Section 4 is rewritten around it.

2Area, rig, method, queue

Four things, and keeping them apart is what makes the rest work.

The area is ground

One parcel, cleared and worked. It holds a handful of slots, and what may stand in them depends on what the ground under it gives. Everything inside an area hands on to everything else for nothing; between areas somebody walks. Section 3.

The rig is capital

A physical thing standing in a slot, built once from materials carried to the area. Its build params are fixed in its masonry: flue height, wall thickness, chamber size, firebox. They set its envelope — the hottest it can ever get, the biggest batch it can hold.

The method is knowledge

A recipe plus its run params, with a name, living in the town's book. Portable: stamp it onto any rig whose envelope can take it. This is the unit of progress — the game is about the book you end up with.

The queue is policy

An ordered list of methods, kept per area rather than per rig, each with a stop condition. A worker takes the first that can run on a rig standing here. One list per area is the whole of what a player manages.

The split means a kiln that cannot reach white heat refuses the lime method in plain words — "needs white; this kiln tops out at orange" — rather than silently making bad lime. It also means the answer to "I need hotter" is sometimes a better method and sometimes a taller flue, and telling those apart is the game.

3Work areas and slots

The map carries work areas, not machines. An area is one parcel with a few slots in it; you fill the slots from a list. Everything standing in the same area hands on to everything else for nothing, and only the gaps between areas need carrying. The map gets simpler and the decisions move inside.

This replaces the works floor — the room of squares and chutes that a process was laid out across. Routing a load from a drop-off to a mill to a pick-up was never the interesting decision, and it put a second, worse puzzle in front of the one this game is actually about. What survives is everything that made it worth having: a process is still a set of stations in order, you still see which of them is stuck, and a works is still a place you go into rather than a menu.

Three views, and each one answers a different question

The same area, at the three distances a player looks at it from. Nothing is repeated between them: what the map can show, the sheet leaves out, and what the sheet can show, the slot leaves out.

What an area is

  • One parcel, so the existing lattice, claims and footpaths are untouched.
  • A few slots. Slots are the scarce thing in the whole design.
  • One shelf: the area's goods, shared by everything standing in it.
  • One door: carriers fetch from and deliver to the area, not to a machine. Dispatch gets much cheaper, since it now weighs pairs of areas rather than pairs of buildings.
  • One list of what is made here, in order, with a stop condition per line.
  • No name. An area is not a kind of building, so there is nothing to call it. What identifies it is what is standing in it, and that is already the first thing on the sheet.

Why the map view has to keep working

A player should be able to read a town without opening anything. While areas are yards that is free — the stations are standing there in the open. Once they are roofed the map has to say the same things another way, and it already knows how: smoke colour at the vent, a glow at the door, a cross over an area that is stuck.

Which means roofing is not a pure upgrade even before the slot costs are counted. Five slots and no sun, and you can no longer see what the place is doing from outside. That is a fair trade and the player should feel it as one.

Laying the sheet out

The first build of this screen put four different kinds of thing at one weight, and the loudest element on it — four lines of percentages for the forage pool — was the least important kind. Space on the sheet is earned by how often a thing is wanted and how fast it changes, not by how much there is to say about it.

  • One column grid for the list: station, inputs, arrow, outputs, aligned across every row. The first build had the station at one edge and the arrow two thirds of the way across with nothing between them, and no two rows agreed where anything sat.
  • Every row the same height. The pooled line was four times the height of the others, which stopped the list reading as a list at all.
  • Show the odds, do not state them. Ten pips in the items' own colours is what ten finds look like: you see that half of it is deadwood without reading anything. Four percentages is a table, and a table of constants has no business outranking the state.
  • Red means something is wrong, and nothing else. A locked slot was drawn as a bright red cross, which is the most alarming mark on the panel spent on the least urgent fact. A slot that is merely not yet available is a quiet dashed patch with a small closed mark; red is kept for the thing that needs somebody to act.
  • A cost is pips that fill, not a fraction. "0/2" beside an unlabelled icon reads as nothing. Two marks that fill as the goods arrive read as a progress you can see across the room, and they match the shelf's own idiom.
  • One kind of border. Filled slots have none — the sprite is the thing. Empty ones are dashed ground. The only bordered control on the sheet is the button that raises the area, because it is the only thing that spends materials.
  • No heading where a rule would do. "Production" was a word doing a job the divider and the grid already do.

One icon has to change for any of this to work: deadwood was drawn as two crossed sticks, which is the same shape as the blocked marker. A row making four deadwood read as "blocked ×4". It is now a stack, and no item icon may ever be an X again.

Why free transfer inside an area is not a cheat

"Nothing teleports" is the project's first rule, and this bends it on purpose, so it has to be fenced. It is fenced four ways, and the first is the one that matters:

  • Slots are few, and the chapter needs about twenty stations. The largest area holds six. No area can ever hold a chain, so the chain is always cut somewhere and somebody always walks.
  • Ground slots are typed by the ground. A clay pit needs a marsh bank, a quarry needs highland, a woodcutter needs forest. Those cannot be in the same area because that parcel cannot be all three things, so geography does most of the cutting for you.
  • Only the walk is free, never the work. A station still takes its own time and still needs a villager when it is a worked one. An area is not a machine that goes faster; it is a yard where nobody has to carry anything across it.
  • Everything in and out still queues through one door. A full area with one door is a real bottleneck, and a second area beside it is a real answer.

The fiction is just people working within arm's reach of each other. Two of them at one fire pass a pot between them; two of them a hundred paces apart need a third to walk it over. That is what the rule has always meant.

Slots, and what fills them

Two kinds, and the split is what keeps geography in the game:

  • Ground slots take a land work, and only one the parcel actually yields. A bank has one or two (water, clay); a plain has earth; highland has stone and limestone. An area with no ground slot is a pure workshop.
  • Work slots take any station the book knows: a clamp, a kiln, a forming bench, a knapping floor.

So siting an area is the same decision it always was — which bank, which wood, which hill — but now it carries a handful of stations with it, and what you choose to put beside the clay pit is a second decision on top.

Two things worth noticing about that ladder. It is paid for in daub, cord, timber, thatch, brick and mortar — which is to say, in the chapter's own products, so the thing you build in order to build more is the thing you were already making. And the top two stages cost as well as give: a roof is five slots but no drying ground, a stone works is six but one is spoken for. An upgrade that is purely better is a decision nobody has to make.

The number to get right is the slot count, and it is the only number here that can wreck the game. Too many and the town is one area and no carriers; too few and the player spends the chapter fighting the cap rather than the craft. Twenty-odd stations against a ceiling of six means at least four areas if perfectly packed, and six to eight in practice once the ground typing and the roof conflict have had their say. Treat two, three, four, five, six as a starting guess and tune it the first time the chapter is played end to end.

4Needs come first

A villager is not free labour. Each one carries three meters — thirst, hunger and rest — that run down on their own, and when one reaches its mark that villager stops taking work and goes to see to it. Nothing is budgeted. The day is whatever they all actually did.

Thirst and hunger are there from the first minute, not deferred to a later age. The band camps on a riverbank because that is where you can drink, and it forages all day because that is what eating costs when you have no tools. Every technology in the chapter is, in the end, an argument about hours.

This is the part the first implementation changed, and changed for the better. The draft worked a town's day out as a budget from its equipment — so many minutes of walking per litre, so many hours of foraging per head. Meters are simpler, they are per villager rather than per town, and they put this system under the same rule as everything else here: watched, not predicted. The day is counted a villager-tick at a time and read back afterwards.

A day as the game recorded it

A town of five in Age I, averaged over twenty days by level 16. A villager's day is a minute of real time — six hundred ticks — and comes to fourteen hours.

Went onHoursShare

About three drinks, two meals and a sleep in it. Those three figures are what the drain rates were tuned against, and level 16 holds them there.

Seeing to a need, and what the chapter sells

Every need has the same shape: the thing in the wild is slow, the thing in a store is quick, and closing that gap is the whole of what the chapter is selling. Nothing here raises a cap or unlocks a tier — it just takes less of the day.

NeedIn the wildOut of a storeComes round

Those are the service times alone. Measured end to end with the walk included, a stored meal ran 34 ticks against 55 for picking your own, and a roofed night 73 against 104 by the fire.

Water, and why the first vessel is a milestone

  • Water cannot be hauled until there is something to carry it in. Until then every villager walks to the bank for every drink and nobody fetches for anybody. A daubed basket holds three litres, a fired pot eight.
  • So the bank dig is the way out of the circle. Mud comes straight out of a wet bank without carrying any water — which is the only reason the first vessel can ever be made. A bank yields water, mud and clay; that is why towns are founded on one.
  • A drink has three tiers: water standing in a building, then a well, then the open bank. The villager takes the nearest that exists, which means a well near the fire quietly pays for itself long before anyone counts.

Food is berries, and they are scarce on purpose

  • The forage camp draws from a pool rather than one item: deadwood seven times in ten, berries two, fibre one. So food arrives as a by-product of gathering fuel, which is exactly how it would.
  • A villager with no berries in store picks their own where something grows — always available, four times slower than eating out of a store. Nobody ever starves for want of a building; they only ever lose the afternoon.
  • One handful fills about a third of the meter, so a meal is a real interruption rather than a tick.

Rest, and the size of the town

Sleep is the one need a building answers outright. A design carries beds and whether those beds are sheltered; a roofed night restores at nearly twice the rate of one by the fire, and somebody asleep indoors is off the map the way a worker on a shift is. A town can be no bigger than the beds it has built, which is what replaced hiring carriers out of thin air.

The rules this system keeps

  • Needs cost time, never lives. A meter at nothing slows nobody and kills nobody; it only means that villager sees to it before anything else. A survival death-loop punishes the player for the one thing they have least information about, and this game is about information.
  • Everybody starts out of step. Fresh meters are spread by who the villager is, because a town where all five stop at once reads as the game having frozen.
  • A drink or a meal comes before sleep, whichever is lower — lie down hungry and hunger wakes you within the hour.
  • Nothing is carried that nobody wants. A store takes anything and is the destination of last resort, so an item no method asks for ends up at the fire for good. With water that is not merely untidy: it could fill the very shelf the sealing method draws its baskets off. An item is cargo only when some method in the book asks for it, or a villager would eat or drink it.
  • Surplus is the score. The share of the town's day that is not survival, always on. In the recorded day above it is 45%, in a town that has barely started.

One thing the draft claimed that cannot be claimed yet: a fifteen-fold growth in spare hours across the chapter. That was a projection from the budget model, and the budget model is gone. Only Age I has been measured. The later ages have to be played before anyone writes a number down for them, which is the same discipline the rest of this document asks for.

5The hand that sets it

A setting is a picture of the state you are choosing, scratched on the same stone the ledger is cut into. The thing itself is the readout. There are no sliders, no percentages and no panels with title bars anywhere in this game.

Draft 2's rig panel was a 2024 settings screen wearing pixel clothes: a damper at 55%, a heat bar, a charge rate of 1.2 a minute. All three are wrong for the same reason — they are numbers about a fire rather than a fire. The project already has the doctrine that fixes this. Nothing hovers over a building; the icons are objects and not symbols; a bay is drawn as the thing and the direction, both in pixels; anything written goes on a tag, which is the one thing in the room that is not of the world. The parameter layer has to obey all of it.

Three levels, one thing on each

With several stations to an area, the panel has to stop being flat or it becomes six panels at once. It splits by the question being asked.

LevelAnswersShowsDoes not show
The mapWhere is everything?One mark per area, its stage, a wisp of smoke if it is working.Anything about what is inside.
The area sheetWhat do we make here?The row of slots as pictures, the ordered list of what is made, the shelf.Knobs, build params, counts per station.
The slotHow is this one set?The station's own panel: its knob strip, what it is, how it was built.The area's list — that is one level up.

The rail is what stops this being a stack of modals. Opening a slot slides its panel in under the row; the row stays put and marks which one is open with the same etched arrow the fire bankings use. Going from the clamp to the kiln is one tap, and there is never more than one panel on screen. The three views are drawn together in section 3.

An empty slot is an empty patch of ground, and tapping it opens the list of what could stand there, filtered to what this ground allows and what the book knows. A slot the area cannot use yet is drawn but struck through, with the stage that would open it.

  • The control and the readout are the same drawing. The strip shows three fires; the kiln becomes the one you pick. Nothing has to be mapped between a widget and a world.
  • Incised, never printed. Every scratch is a dark groove with its far wall catching the light down and to the right — the same top-left sun the map is lit by, and the same rule the ledger's lettering already follows.
  • Dimming is the only state cue. An option not in use sits back toward the colour of the stone. No outlines, no greys that are not in the palette.
  • No text inside the world. The figures in this section carry no words at all. Names belong on a tag, and a tag is for things that genuinely have no picture.
  • The surface ages with the town. Sandstone while it is a fire and a few works, a beaten sheet once it is a village, a chalkboard once it builds in stone — the stages render/townStage.ts already turns over at.

One consequence worth stating plainly: some figures in this document are what the player sees, and some are what we know. The response curves in sections 7 to 9 are the second kind — they are the model laid bare for a designer, and the player never sees a curve until section 10 earns it. Each caption says which it is. Two of them sit awkwardly in between — the instrument comparison in section 11 and the sweep's book in section 12 are drawn here as labelled tables because a reader needs to see the whole argument at once. In game neither has a word in it: the book is tallies and drawn marks cut into the town's slab, as in sections 12 and 13, and nobody ever sees three instruments side by side, only the one they own.

6Build params and run params

Every knob is one or the other, and which one it is is a physical question: can you change it without rebuilding?

  • Build params are set once, when the rig is raised, and cost materials: flue height, wall thickness, chamber volume, vent area, firebox size. Changing one means tearing it down.
  • Run params are set any time, free: fuel type, charge rate, damper, stacking density, hold time, and the schedule.
  • A method declares the envelope it needs. Rigs that fall short list what is missing.
  • No knob exists before its part does. There is no damper until somebody builds one, and until then air is whatever the vents give.

7Heat: one model under every fire

The hearth, the pit firing, the clamp and both kilns are the same five lines. Nothing is special-cased, and everything the design claims about fuel and air falls out of it rather than being asserted.

// air the stack can pull — a hotter fire pulls harder, so this is solved by iteration
draught = vent × damper × √(flue × (T − ambient))
burn    = min(charge_rate, air ÷ air_per_fuel)   // short of air, the rest leaves as smoke
heat    = burn × fuel_energy × efficiency        // wood 15 MJ/kg · charcoal 30
flow    = air + burn + unburnt × 2.2             // everything going up the flue
T       = ambient + heat ÷ (flow × c_air + k_wall)   // k_wall ∝ chamber ÷ thickness

Three things worth noticing, because each one is a design claim the model makes true for free:

  • Charcoal is not a free upgrade. Twice the energy of wood per unit, but it needs nearly twice the air. Switching a kiln to charcoal before it has the draught to feed it makes it colder, and it smokes. The player discovers the flue by being punished for it.
  • Draught is self-reinforcing, which is the only reason a damper has to exist. Without one, a kiln that gets hot runs away.
  • The firebox caps the ceiling. Without it, every rig could be stoked to the same temperature and build params would not matter.

8Charring: one knob that disagrees with itself

A clamp is not a fire in a chamber, it is a pile cooking itself: the heat that drives the charring comes from burning a part of the wood. So the one knob — how much air is let in — sets the temperature, and the temperature sets both how much charcoal is left and how good it is, in opposite directions.

Keep the vents nearly shut and you get a lot of half-burnt brands, which are useless for anything that needs real heat. Open them and the pile burns hotter, gives proper charcoal, and gives less of it. That is the whole decision, and it was the colliers' decision too.

Charcoal grade is read by the kiln as a fuel: brands have less energy and need less air, so a lazy clamp quietly lowers the ceiling of every kiln in town. That is the link that makes an early-age rig still matter in Age V.

9The clay web

Clay is where tweaking a lot of parameters pays off most, because the knobs fight. Everything that makes a pot strong also makes it likelier to crack, and temper is the answer to that at the cost of the strength it bought.

Two runs of the same clay, at the same peak, differing only in the settings:

MethodLostHardnessGradeCarriesTrips

Read the rows as two separate lessons. The second row is not a harder pot than the first — it is the same pot, and nearly all of them came out of the kiln instead of nearly none. Hardness is bought later, by the soak in the third row, and it is read downstream: a hard pot carries more water per trip and survives more trips before it breaks. That is how a firing schedule ends up deciding how many people have to walk to the river.

10Knapping, with the clock taken off

What was good about the knapping game was reading a shape and choosing a point. That is a spatial parameter and it never needed a timer. So the core stays a persistent object you can put down and come back to, and each blow is four values:

  • Where on the edge — pointed at directly, which is the part that felt good.
  • Platform angle, which has to lean under 90° or the blow slides off.
  • Hammer: hard stone, soft stone or antler.
  • Force, and whether the platform was prepared first.

A long thin flake is a better edge and takes more of the core with it, so the trade is edge quality against how many edges one stone gives. Antler makes beautiful flakes and almost exhausts the core; a hard hammer is wasteful of quality and generous with quantity. Which you want depends on whether you are making a woodcutter's axe or forty cheap scrapers.

11Knobs and instruments

This is the idea that makes the whole design feel like discovery rather than a settings screen. Early on there is no temperature readout anywhere in the game. There is a glowing chamber, and glow colour tells you roughly where you are: dull red is around 600, cherry around 750, orange 900, yellow past 1000. That is a band a hundred and fifty degrees wide, and you tune inside it by guessing.

An instrument narrows the band. A shrinkage test ring pulled from the chamber tells you within about seventy degrees. A pyrometric cone either bends or it does not, which pins you to fifteen. The fog is over your knowledge, and lifting it is the progress.

Consequence worth being deliberate about: the same method run by a player with cones is reliably better than one run by eye, because they can actually hit the band they wrote down. Instruments are a real upgrade without adding a single new material.

A knob is only as fine as the instrument that reads it

Draft 2 drew continuous sliders and response curves from the first minute, which contradicts everything above: nobody can set a damper to 62% before anybody can measure anything. So the control has to evolve with the sense, not just the readout.

The rule this buys: never draw a line between two settings the player has no reason to believe anything lies between. Three settings are three bars. A curve is a claim about the space between the points, and that claim needs an instrument behind it.

And the output is watched, not predicted

There is no preview of what a setting will yield. You pick one, the rig runs, and the town reports what came out — as a rate, over time: about three a day, give or take a lot. Run it again and the estimate tightens.

That makes waiting meaningful rather than dead time, and it makes the early game quietly honest: a method is a thing you believe on evidence, not a number the game handed you. The spread is floored by the instrument, so by eye alone it never gets better than about a fifth either way, however long you watch.

The variation is not random. It comes from real state — damp fuel, a coarser batch of clay, a slower worker — so the tick stays deterministic and two runs of the same seed still agree, which keeps level 4's trace hash intact.

12Trials, sweeps and the book

A trial is a queue entry like any other, but it runs a sweep: the same method at several values of one parameter, one small batch each, writing a row into the book per value. It costs a worker and a fifth of the materials a real batch would.

A sweep moves one parameter. Two at once is a grid, costs twenty-five batches and turns the book into a mess. Forcing one at a time is both real experimental discipline and good pacing: it keeps a session's discoveries legible.

When the book holds enough rows to show a pattern, the game names it. That is the only form of tech unlock in the design — no nodes, no points. You can also just read the table and work it out yourself first, in which case naming it only makes the readout explicit.

Nothing is ever blocked on tuning. Every rig opens with a working default method, so a player who never runs a trial still gets a functioning town — just a wasteful one.

13What the area makes

The list belongs to the area, not to a rig — one list to manage however many stations stand here. Each line names a method and is bound to the station that provides it, which is drawn beside it. A worker starting a shift scans down the list and takes the first that can run: inputs on the shelf, room for the output, stock below its mark, and a free rig here whose envelope satisfies it. The scan is in declaration order, so it stays deterministic and the trace hash holds.

Two stations of the same kind mean two lines of the same method, and they run side by side. That is the only place parallelism enters, and it is visible rather than inferred.

Each entry carries a stop condition, or the first one starves everything below it forever. A stock target is the honest form: make this until we have N.

  • Changeover costs on a fire rig. Switching from lime to brick means the fire comes down and goes back up — thirty to sixty seconds. That one number pushes the player into firing campaigns: all the lime, then all the brick, which is how kilns were actually run. On a knapping floor the changeover is zero.
  • Capacity is concurrent batches: a kiln has one chamber, a knapping floor has room for three.
  • The list is the only thing a player manages per area. Knobs live one level down, in the slot. That split is what keeps a six-slot area from being six panels.
  • Crew is people per batch, as a range. A kiln takes one to two — a second stoker speeds the climb but not the soak. A clamp takes up to three while it is being built and one to watch it.
  • So three knappers can share one floor on three different methods, while two stokers share one kiln on one.

15The chapter, as methods

The 27 steps survive as a spine, but the verbs change. Five ages, each opening on a new kind of thing. rig is something you build with knobs on it, method is a new recipe for a rig you already have, site is a decision on the map, build is a stockpile carried to a plot, and sense is an instrument.

Slices, in order

  1. Rigs, methods and the queue, with one knob. Charge rate only, on the hearth. Proves the three-layer split and the fall-through before any model exists.
  2. The heat model and the pit firing. Damper, the air peak, the glow readout. This is the point to judge whether tuning feels like understanding.
  3. Trials, sweeps and the book. Including the fog: no numbers until an instrument says so.
  4. Clay and fracture, then the ages in order.

18Open questions

What is the right slot count?

The one number that can wreck this. Two through six is a guess fitted to twenty-odd stations; it needs a full play-through before anyone trusts it. Watch for the town collapsing into two fat areas with no carriers walking between them — that is the failure mode.

Can a station be moved between areas?

Tearing down and rebuilding costs materials, which is honest but punishing when the player realises their layout is wrong at step 20. A cheaper "lift and carry" for hand rigs, with the fired ones staying put, is probably the kinder rule.

How many knobs before it is a spreadsheet?

The kiln has four run params and four build params. That is near the ceiling of what can be read as a place rather than a form. If a later rig wants more, it should probably be two rigs.

Does a method belong to a town or a world?

Per town makes a second settlement start ignorant, which is a real cost and a real story. Per world makes expansion painless. Leaning per town, with methods copyable by a traveller.

Can a bad default stall a new player?

The defaults must be mediocre but never failing. A default that cracks half the ware teaches nothing because nothing comes out to compare against.

Building decay

Without it, mortar ratio and thatch pitch are one-off decisions. With it they become upkeep, and the town ages. It is the cheapest way to keep Age II rigs alive in Age V — but it is also the easiest way to make a game feel like chores.

Seasons

Needs make a winter mean something: forage yield collapses, fuel demand doubles, and preserved food is the only answer. It is the natural next pressure and it is also the fastest way to make the chapter frustrating. Probably chapter two.

Farming

Foraging has a ceiling, and the pyramid says the next move is to raise food per hour rather than find more ground. That is the whole of chapter two and it should not be smuggled into this one.

How visible is the hour budget?

One always-on surplus readout, or a breakdown per person? The first is a score and risks being optimised blindly; the second is honest but is four more numbers on screen. Leaning on the single readout, with the breakdown a click away.
